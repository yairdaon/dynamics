\chapter{The Basic SIR Model}

\section{Compartments}

We assume infection is \emph{acute}: an individual is infected, then
is removed (recovers or dies) and stays removed. Three compartments:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Susceptible: can become infected.
  \item Infected: currently infectious.
  \item Removed: no longer infectious, not reinfected.
\end{itemize}

We write $X,Y,Z$ for the \emph{number} of individuals in each class
(this choice of notation will become clear later). The total
population size $N$ is assumed constant.

\section{Recovery: an exponential process}

Assume individual recovery happens at a constant rate $\gamma$, and
for now ignore new infections. A constant recovery rate means each
individual's remaining infectious time $T$ is exponential:
$T\sim\text{Exp}(\gamma)$.

The probability that an individual is still infected (not yet
recovered) at time $t$ is
\[
P(T>t) = \int_t^\infty \gamma e^{-\gamma s}\,ds = e^{-\gamma t}.
\]
Let $Y_0:=Y(0)$. Since each of the $Y_0$ initial infecteds recovers
independently with this same law,
\[
Y_t = Y_0\,e^{-\gamma t},
\]
at least on average. Differentiating,
\[
\dot Y_t = \frac{d}{dt}\left(Y_0 e^{-\gamma t}\right) = -\gamma Y_0 e^{-\gamma t} = -\gamma Y_t.
\]
So with recovery alone, $\dot Y = -\gamma Y$.

\paragraph{What does $\gamma$ mean?}
$\gamma$ is the per-capita recovery rate (this is just a fancy way of
saying ``individual recovery rate''). Since the infectious period is
$T\sim\text{Exp}(\gamma)$, the mean infectious period is
$1/\gamma$. Example: if infected individuals recover after 5 days on
average, $\gamma = 1/5\ \text{day}^{-1}$.

\section{Adding new infections}

New infections add to $Y$; recoveries remove from $Y$:
\[
\dot Y = (\text{rate of new infections}) - \underbrace{\gamma Y}_{\text{recovery rate}}.
\]
Let us assume for now that there are no recoveries. We derive the rate
of new infections from first principles. Let $\kappa$ denote the
number of contacts per time unit for a susceptible individual. So
during a short time interval $\delta t$ this individual has $\kappa
\frac{Y}{N} \times \delta t$ contacts with infecteds. We let
\[
c = \Pr(\text{infection}|\text{contact with infected}).
\]

Hence $1-c$ is the probability of the complement (no infection given a
contact) and by example \ref{example:no infection}:
\begin{align*}
  \Pr(\text{no infection during time $\delta t$}) &= (1-c)^{\kappa \frac{Y}{N} \delta t} \\
  &= e^{-\beta \frac{Y}{N} \delta t} \text{ (we let $\beta:= -\kappa \log(1-c)$)} 
\end{align*}
and so
\begin{align*}
  \Pr(\text{infection during time $\delta t$})
  %&= 1 - (1-c)^{\kappa \frac{Y}{N} \delta t} \\
  &= 1 - e^{-\beta \frac{Y}{N} \delta t}\\
  %%\quad  (\text{let $\beta:= -\kappa \log(1-c)$})\\
  &\approx 1 - (1-\beta \frac{Y}{N} \delta t) \quad  (\text{since $e^x \approx 1+x$ for small x})\\
  &= \beta \frac{Y}{N} \delta t.  \\
\end{align*}

\begin{definition}
The \emph{force of infection} (FOI) $\lambda_t$ is the per-capita
rate at which susceptibles become infected.
\end{definition}

We now find the force of infection. First note that
\begin{align*}
  \text{number of new infections during $\delta t$} &= X
  \Pr(\text{infection during time $\delta t$}) \\ &= \beta \frac{Y}{N}
  \delta t \cdot X,
\end{align*}
so the rate of new infections is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:new infections}
\text{rate of new infections} = \frac{\beta \frac{Y}{N} \delta
  t}{\delta t} X = \beta \frac{Y}{N} X.
\end{equation}
Therefore, in our case $\lambda_t = \beta \frac{Y}{N}$\footnote{This
specific form is called a mass-action force of infection.}.


\paragraph{What does $\beta$ mean?}
By eq.~\eqref{eq:new infections}, the rate of new infections is $\beta
\frac{Y}{N}X$.  Suppose $X= N-1$, i.e. the entire population is
susceptible, except for a single infected individual. Then this rate
is
\begin{align*}
  \beta \frac{Y}{N}X &= \frac{N-1}{N} \beta Y \\
  %
  %
  &= \frac{N-1}{N} \beta \text{ (taking $Y=1$)} \\
  %
  %
  &\approx \beta, 
\end{align*}
where the last approximation holds for a large population. Thus, a
single infected generates new infections at rate $\beta$ per time
unit, provided the entire population is susceptible.

\paragraph{What assumptions did we make?}
Contacts do not depend on the infection state of
individuals; the number of contacts between individuals does not
depend on population size $N$: doubling both $N$ and $Y$ leaves
$\lambda_t$ unchanged.


\section{Putting everything together}
We dropped recoveries above only to isolate the derivation of the
infection rate; reinstating the recovery term $\gamma Y$ we have
\begin{align*}
\dot{X} &= -\text{rate of new infections} = -\beta \frac{Y}{N} X, \\
\dot{Y} &= \text{rate of new infections} - \text{rate of recovery} = \beta\frac{Y}{N}X - \gamma Y, \\
\dot{Z} &= \text{rate of recovery} = \gamma Y.
\end{align*}

Dividing each equation by the population size $N$ (a constant), and
writing $S=X/N$, $I=Y/N$, $R=Z/N$ for the population proportions gives
the standard formulation of the SIR model:
\begin{align*}
\dot S &= -\beta S I, \\
\dot I &= \beta S I - \gamma I, \\
\dot R &= \gamma I.
\end{align*}

Note $\dot S+\dot I+\dot R=0$: since $S,I,R$ are proportions,
$S+I+R=1$ is constant. Hence one equation is redundant --- given any
two, the third follows. It is common to drop the $\dot R$ equation and
recover $R=1-S-I$ when needed.
